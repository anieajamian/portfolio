## 2. Medly page

The strongest case study on the site, currently framed as a healthcare project.
It is a fulfillment project.

### 2a. Role field

Currently "Lead Product Designer, B2C Web." The resume and LinkedIn both say
Senior Product Designer. Pick one and use it in all three places. If the working
title was Lead, use "Senior Product Designer (design lead, B2C web)" so the
documents reconcile.

### 2b. Impact statement — reorder

Currently opens with agent time freed, which is internal efficiency. Lead with
the scale number.

> Scaled scheduled deliveries from 20–30 to 250+ per day company-wide (1,000+
> prescriptions in peak weeks) and freed ~65% of patient-experience agents' time,
> by moving prescription scheduling off the phone into a self-serve flow.

### 2c. New section — insert after Overview, before The Problem

Heading: **The System**

> Medly ran a set of intertwined products: the patient app, courier dispatch,
> pharmacist fulfillment tooling, and tooling for patient-experience agents. I
> owned the patient-facing checkout. Every scheduling choice a patient made
> landed downstream on a courier route, a pharmacist's fill queue, or an agent's
> escalation path — so the flow had to hold against constraints from all three
> teams, none of which I controlled.
>
> **[NEED]** One concrete example: the hardest constraint another team placed on
> the design. Courier batching windows, pharmacist fill cutoffs, an agent
> escalation requirement, a compliance rule. One sentence naming the constraint
> and one naming how the design absorbed it.

Why this matters: it is the difference between "healthcare designer" and
"designer who has worked in a multi-sided operation." The 65% agent-time number
is already proof — you cannot free two thirds of an internal team's capacity by
changing a consumer flow unless you understood the operation you were designing
against. Right now the site presents the result without the reasoning.

### 2d. Constraints section — active voice

Current: "Rather than ship a frustrating handoff, we delayed public launch to
build the full checkout experience natively, and phased lower-priority pieces
for later."

This is the strongest scope moment in the case study and it reads as something
that happened nearby. A mid-project launch delay on quality grounds, with a
re-scoping plan attached, is exactly what senior interviews probe for.

**[NEED]** Did you make that call, argue for it, or was it decided above you? If
you drove it, say so:

> When engineering found mid-build that the two systems couldn't sync patient
> data, I made the case for delaying public launch rather than shipping a handoff
> that would have made patients re-enter information we already had — and
> re-scoped the release so the lower-priority pieces shipped in a later phase.

If you argued for it without owning the decision, "I pushed for" is accurate and
still strong. If it came from above, leave it as-is and cut this note.

### 2e. Pull exception handling out of the footer

The closing currently reads: "Testing also surfaced the post-launch roadmap
patients asked for directly: order cancellation and rescheduling, default payment
methods, and richer delivery notifications."

Cancellation, rescheduling, and delivery notifications *are* order quality. They
are the answer to "what happens when an order needs to change or goes wrong,"
and they are buried under a roadmap label at the bottom of the page.

Give them a short section before Impact, headed **When Orders Change**:

> Usability testing surfaced a consistent second question: not "how do I schedule
> this" but "what happens when something goes wrong." Patients wanted to cancel
> and reschedule deliveries themselves, save a default payment method, and get
> richer notifications about where their order actually was.
>
> **[NEED]** Did you design any of this — a status state, an error message, a
> cancellation path, a delivery notification? Even a single screen. If yes, show
> it here. If no, keep the section but end with the roadmap framing.

Keep the closing note about patients who still preferred walking to the pharmacy.
It shows restraint and it is one of the few places the writing has a point of
view.
